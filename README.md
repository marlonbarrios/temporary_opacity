# Temporary Opacity

by Marlon Barrios Solano January 28th 2026

Alex worked mornings at a vegan coffee shop in Gainesville that smelled like oat milk, disinfectant, and ambition.

The espresso machine hissed like it was always slightly offended. Students ordered ethically and anxiously, as if consumption itself had become a test they were constantly failing. Outside, Spanish moss hung from live oaks like exhausted punctuation, soft and gray and unmoving in the heat. Across the street, two storefronts were empty—windows papered over, old logos still faintly visible, as if businesses now lingered longer as ghosts than as operations.

Down the block, a plasma lab opened early.

By seven a.m., people gathered there quietly—students mostly, some older, some too young-looking for the seriousness of the transaction. They stood in line with earbuds in, hoodies pulled tight despite the heat, waiting to exchange an hour of their blood for grocery money, rent, or just time. The sign promised *donations*, but everyone knew it was a sale. A small economy of veins and plastic chairs, unfolding every morning before class.

Gainesville had learned to monetize endurance.

It was 2025.

Trump had returned to government, and nobody called it a return. They called it a *correction*.

Alex was finishing his last semester in sociology.

Born in Cuba.  
Raised there.  
Arrived in the United States fifteen years ago with his parents and a plastic folder full of documents and family stories that did not agree with each other.

In class, he spoke Marx fluently.

Alienation.  
Commodity fetishism.  
Surplus value.

He believed it. Mostly.

And yet his body rejected the word *communism* every time someone pronounced it with academic nostalgia. Cuba was not theory. Cuba was queues and whispers and the slow education of fear. It was learning how to lower your voice before you knew why.

He hated the regime.  
He hated American capitalism’s performance of innocence.  
He hated that both systems demanded loyalty while offering control.

Contradiction wasn’t a failure.  
It was training.

At night, Alex was a DJ.

Techno sets that were clean and relentless, four-on-the-floor like a body insisting it still existed. But at home—alone or with friends—he played old Spanish pop from before everything became political. Strange ballads. Synthetic strings. Excess feeling without apology.

Music was where he allowed incoherence.

He dressed like a question.

Baggy pants that collapsed around his ankles. Cropped t-shirts that revealed just enough to confuse people. Sometimes nail polish. Sometimes not. He liked the way certain men looked at him—hesitant, curious, unsure what category he belonged to.

Alex liked being unreadable.

He had a girlfriend.

Three years.

Long enough to feel permanent.  
Long enough to feel infrastructural.

She talked about Chicago as if it were already happening. The music scene. The future. The way cities made people more *themselves*.

Alex nodded.

He told her he wanted to go.

He did not tell her that part of him wanted to go alone—not to escape her, but to encounter himself without continuity.

Sometimes he thought about men.

Not in images.  
In sensation.

The memory of his father’s voice arrived without warning:

*Prefiero una hija puta que un hijo maricón.*

Alex did not translate it anymore.  
He felt it as pressure.

Immigration tightened.

Friends disappeared from group chats.  
Visas stalled.  
Stories ended mid-sentence.

Alex was white enough to pass.

Until he spoke.

His English was precise.  
Too careful.

The accent slipped out when he was tired or excited, like a watermark he could not erase.

The professor noticed him before Alex noticed the professor.

An artist.  
Three times his age.  
Queer without performance.

He taught a seminar called **Systems That Watch You Back**.

They spoke after class three times. Enough to matter.

“You’re a Marxist who doesn’t trust Marxists,” the professor said once, not unkindly.

Alex laughed.

“That’s not a contradiction,” the professor said. “That’s historical literacy.”

They talked about AI.

Not the demos.  
The infrastructure.

“How power moved from factories to platforms,” the professor said. “From labor to prediction. Surveillance capitalism doesn’t care what you believe. It only cares that you’re legible.”

He spoke about borders becoming datasets. About freedom turning into a permissions problem. About governance without ideology.

“You don’t need fascism anymore,” the professor said. “You just need optimization.”

“And Cuba?” Alex asked.

The professor paused.

“Different aesthetics,” he said. “Same fantasy. Total knowledge. Total control. Different branding.”

Alex felt something then.

Attraction, yes.  
But also relief.

No line was crossed.

That, Alex understood, was part of the intelligence.

Graduation arrived quietly.

No revelation.  
No clarity.

Just deadlines and paperwork.

Before Chicago, Alex flew to Miami.

His parents’ apartment smelled like coffee and old habits. The television stayed too loud, cycling between panic and celebration. His mother touched his face like she was checking for damage.

“You’re thin,” she said.

“I’m fine.”

His father asked about work. About Chicago. About money.

They did not ask about politics.  
They did not ask about clothes.  
They did not ask about desire.

At night, Alex walked alone through neighborhoods that felt both familiar and foreign. Spanish everywhere. Surveillance everywhere. Cameras no longer pretending not to see.

He thought about the professor’s word:

*Legibility.*

He packed again.

Records.  
Clothes.  
Books he would pretend to reread.

At the airport, his girlfriend texted:

*Can’t wait to start our life.*

Alex stared at the message longer than necessary.

He boarded the plane.

Chicago waited.  
His girlfriend waited.  
The music scene waited.

As the plane lifted, Gainesville flattened into grids, parking lots, abandoned plazas—storefronts emptied out faster than the signs announcing their optimism. The city dissolved into heat, trees, and soft decay.

Then clouds.

White.  
Thick.  
Indifferent.

Alex felt the pressure in his chest—not fear, not relief, but something closer to suspension.

Not arrival.  
Not escape.

Suspended.

He thought of blood in plastic bags.  
Of data in servers.  
Of bodies turned into resources.

He thought of systems that watched you back.

Up here, above borders and datasets, above accent and ideology, he felt briefly unreadable.

Neither Marxist nor anti-communist.  
Neither straight nor not.  
Neither citizen nor threat.

Just a body in transit.

He did not feel free.  
He did not feel trapped.

He felt temporary opacity—not the absence of meaning, but the refusal of resolution.

Alex closed his eyes.

Chicago would demand choices.  
His girlfriend would demand clarity.  
The world would demand alignment.

But for now, in this narrow interval between ground and ground, he allowed himself not to know.

Which, he realized, might be the most honest position he had left.
